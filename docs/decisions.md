# Data decisions

Choices the spec did not settle, recorded when they were made. Phase 0 entries were written before any pageview was fetched.

## Phase 0: the event list (frozen 2026-10-28)

1. **Death dates.** Only day-precision dates of death count, and deprecated Wikidata statements are skipped. 6 people whose date is given only to the month or year, and 33 whose item carries two different day-precision dates, are dropped before the list is written (`data/events_dropped_dates.json`).
2. **The sitelink floor is measured now.** Wikidata reports today's sitelink count, not the count on the day someone died. A person who gained articles in other languages after dying can clear the floor of 30 because of the death itself.
3. **"Created within 30 days before the event" includes articles created after it.** Either way no baseline exists. 26 events fall under this rule (21 deaths, 5 awards), among them George Floyd, Mahsa Amini and Li Wenliang, and Nobel laureates whose English article was written on the day of the prize.
4. **Award dates.** Nobel Prizes use `dateAwarded` from the Nobel Prize API (the announcement day). Academy Awards use the date of the ceremony item each win points to on Wikidata. The Ballon d'Or uses the ceremony date in the infobox of each year's English Wikipedia article, and the Booker Prize the announcement sentence in each year's article ("Man Booker Prize" up to 2018). The source is recorded per row in `date_source`.
5. **Ballon d'Or means the men's award.** The spec names "the Ballon d'Or"; the Ballon d'Or Féminin is a separate award and is not included. There was no award in 2020.
6. **The 2025 Booker Prize** was announced on 10 November 2025, after the window closes on 31 October 2025, so it is not in the list.
7. **Nobel Peace Prizes to organizations** are left out; only people count.
8. **Repeat winners** (Messi three times, Ronaldo, McDormand, Stone and Ali twice each) are separate events. Ronaldo's 2017 award falls inside the year after his 2016 one.
9. **People in both lists.** Seven Nobel laureates in the list have died since their prize, all more than a year after it, so their award events stay. Six of those deaths fall inside the window and are death events too.
10. **Occupation groups.** Each occupation on a person's Wikidata item is traced up the subclass tree to a fixed set of roots (`scripts/fetch_events.py`, `GROUPS`). A person takes the group of the first listed occupation that maps anywhere; an occupation reaching several roots takes the earliest group in this order: sport, politics and law, screen, music, writing and journalism, science and academia, business, visual arts and design, religion. Anyone unmatched is "other".
11. **Age at event** uses the earliest day-precision birth date; a birth date given only to the year is placed on 1 July, and to the month on the 15th.

## Phase 1: pageviews

12. **User-Agent.** Requests carry `fame-half-life/1.0 (https://github.com/lyhjeremy/fame-half-life; https://lyhjeremy.com)`. A first string ending in `python-urllib` was throttled with HTTP 429 within minutes; the run was stopped, the string changed, and the request gap raised to 0.5 seconds across all threads.
13. **Renames.** A page move leaves a revision whose summary reads "moved page [[A]] to [[B]]". Any such revision between day -60 and day +365 drops the event. The first pass checked only the window and turned up 7 events with no data at all. Their articles had been moved after the window, so the current title had no views back then. The check now runs on to the fetch date. When an article was moved after its window, every title in that later move history is a candidate. The views come from whichever title drew the most during the window, since the others were redirects or did not exist yet. A rule that simply took the source of the earliest later move picked a vandal's title twice, where the vandal's own move had been suppressed from the history.
14. **Missing days.** The pageview API leaves out days with no views; those are filled with 0. Days after 27 October 2026, the last complete day on the fetch date, are left empty.

## Phase 2: measures and fits

15. **Day-of-week adjustment.** Factors are the mean views on each weekday in the baseline window divided by the window mean, clipped to 0.5 to 2 and rescaled to average 1. Every day's views are divided by its weekday's factor before any measure is taken.
16. **Half-life is counted from the peak day,** the day of most views in days 0 to +3, and is the number of days until the centered 7-day mean of excess views first falls below half the peak's excess.
17. **A visible spike** means peak views at least twice the baseline. Events below that have no half-life and no curve fit, and are counted as "no visible spike".
18. **Curve fits** use the days after the peak day up to day +365, on days where excess views are above zero (a log needs a positive number). An event needs 30 such days to be fitted. AIC is computed from the residual sum of squares of log excess, so each model is compared on the same days.
19. **The biexponential** is `A (w e^(-t/tau1) + (1 - w) e^(-t/tau2))`, the functional form of Candia et al. (2019), fitted by a fixed grid plus Nelder-Mead, so the browser can run the same code. It has four parameters against two for the exponential and the power law.
20. **Lift near the end of the window.** Events in the last days of October 2025 do not reach day +365 by 27 October 2026. Lift is computed when at least 21 of days +335 to +365 exist.
21. **Baseline under 1 view a day** leaves no ratio to compute; such events are dropped and counted.
22. **Bot check.** Any day above 50 times the median of its six neighbors (three each side) is cut to that bound, and every measure is recomputed.
23. **A second fade measure.** On the first few hundred events the half-life as specified came out at one day almost every time, because the spike is narrower than the 7-day smoothing window. It stays the reported half-life. Next to it the analysis reports the share of the first year's excess views (counted from the peak day) that arrive more than 7 days after the peak. That share varies from event to event, so it carries the group comparisons. The averaged curve's half and tenth points and the fitted time constants are reported too.
24. **No SciPy.** The spec lists SciPy for the curve fits. The fits use a fixed grid and a hand-written Nelder-Mead instead, so `assets/fit.js` can run exactly the same steps in the browser. `scripts/check_fit_parity.py` confirms the two agree.
25. **Network faults are retried, never recorded.** A connection reset during the pageview run stopped it once. The cache stores only successful answers and genuine HTTP 404s, so the rerun picked up where it stopped. Any reset, timeout or DNS failure is retried.

## Post-audit corrections (2026-10-29)

An independent audit of the version published on 28 October found the problems below. Each entry gives the change and the reason. Where a fix departs from the spec, the spec's measure is still reported as a sensitivity row. Every figure on the pages comes from the corrected run; the old figures are named in the corrections note.

26. **Three missing laureates (data bug, not a rule change).** The Nobel Prize API gives `dateAwarded` 2011-10-10 for the 2022 Economic Sciences prize, so Bernanke, Diamond and Dybvig fell outside the window and were never listed. `fetch_events.py` now checks that `dateAwarded` falls in the prize year and stops if it does not. The 2018 Literature prize, announced in October 2019, is the one legitimate exception. It also uses 10 October 2022 for this prize, the date of the nobelprize.org press release cited in the three laureates' English Wikipedia articles. The frozen list is not regenerated. `scripts/amend_events.py` appends the three rows (event ids a2183 to a2185), marks the correction in their `date_source`, and leaves every frozen row unchanged.
27. **Model comparison refitted; every day counts.** The first fit took logs of excess views and dropped every day at or below the baseline. That throws away exactly the days that tell a fast exponential from a slow tail. On simulated pure exponentials it picked the biexponential 100% of the time (`tables/model_recovery.csv`, column `spec_method_picked_biexp`). The fit is now log(1 + views) against log(1 + b + f(t)) on every day after the peak, with b the measured baseline and f the exponential, power-law or biexponential excess. Parameters start from a fixed grid and are refined by Nelder-Mead, with the same code in Python and the browser. `scripts/simulate_selection.py` validates it on simulated series with known decay, weekly cycles and Poisson-lognormal noise (sigma 0.15 and 0.30). It names the true model 82 to 87% of the time for a pure exponential, 88 to 95% for a power law and 99 to 100% for a biexponential. It calls 13 to 17% of pure exponentials biexponential, so the real-data biexponential share is somewhat overstated, and the page says so. The spec's method stays in `tables/model_wins.csv` (the `spec_` columns).
28. **Half-life compares like with like.** The spec's half-life compared a 7-day centered mean with a single raw peak day, so it came out at one day almost every time. The main half-life now compares the smoothed series with its own maximum over days 0 to 3, counted from the day of that maximum. The raw-daily version (daily excess against the daily peak) and the spec's version are sensitivity rows in `tables/half_life_sensitivity.csv`, with 3- and 14-day smoothing and a 0-to-30-day peak window.
29. **Delayed spikes.** Most deaths first counted as "no visible spike" did spike, days later, when the news broke (Leonard Cohen, Sam Shepard, Steve Ditko). The event date stays the date of death, so the frozen list is unchanged. An event with no spike on days 0 to 3 but a day above twice the baseline on days 4 to 30 is now classed as a delayed spike. Delayed spikes are counted separately and are not fitted. A sensitivity run searches for the peak over days 0 to 30.
30. **Oscar baseline before the nominations.** Days -60 to -8 before an Academy Awards ceremony fall after the nominations, when the winners are already in the news. For the Academy Awards the baseline, and the day-of-week factors, now come from the 53 days ending 8 days before that year's nominations were announced. The dates are in `data/academy_nominations.csv`, each from the ceremony's English Wikipedia article with the press source it cites. The Academy's own site returned HTTP 403 to this project's requests, so the dates are not taken from oscars.org directly. The spec baseline and a third window (days -150 to -90 before the ceremony, the auditor's alternative) are sensitivity rows in `tables/oscar_baseline.csv`. Even the new window falls in early awards season (the Golden Globes and critics' prizes). The page reports all three lifts and says the choice matters.
31. **Copy rewritten to match.** "The fade has two speeds", "two exponentials fit better than one", the description of the slow component as a flat step, "an Oscar leaves it quieter" and "depends on how known the winner was" were removed or rewritten from the corrected numbers. A dated corrections note is on the landing page, in the write-up and in `writeup.md`.
32. **Series files not committed.** `data/series.csv.gz` duplicated `assets/series/` and went against the no-datasets convention. It is gitignored, along with the Oscar baseline windows, and `fetch_pageviews.py` rebuilds both from the response cache. `data/fetch_log.csv` and the event list stay committed.
33. **Smaller fixes.** Every page declares an empty favicon, so browsers don't request `/favicon.ico`. American spelling in code (`organizations`, `analyze`). `nDropDates` and the last data day are read from `data/events_dropped_dates.json` and `results/summary.json` instead of being typed. The page script attaches one resize listener instead of one per lookup.
