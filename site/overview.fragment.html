The question

When someone becomes news, how fast does the world stop looking them up?

A death or a prize sends a burst of readers to a Wikipedia article. This study measures that burst for every person with an English article and at least 30 Wikidata sitelinks who died between January 2016 and October 2025 ({{nDeaths}} after exclusions), and for the winners of six Nobel Prizes, five Academy Awards, the Ballon d'Or and the Booker Prize in the same years ({{nAwards}}). For each event it reads daily views from 60 days before to a year after, from Wikimedia's pageview API, counting user agents only.

The event list was fixed and committed before a single pageview was fetched, so nothing about the results could shape who is in it. {{nCreatedDeath}} deaths and {{nCreatedAward}} awards drop out because the article was written less than 30 days before the event and has no baseline; among them are George Floyd and Mahsa Amini, whose deaths made them known. {{nRenamed}} more drop because the article was renamed inside the window, which splits its views across two titles.

Limits

What this cannot show

English Wikipedia only, and a pageview is someone looking a person up, which is not the same as caring about them. The 30-sitelink floor decides who counts as famous and leans toward people known in many countries; it is today's count, so a few people clear it because of the death itself. Some automated traffic gets past the user filter. A death or prize can coincide with other news about the same person, a film release or a scandal, and nothing here separates the two. Awards season raises an Oscar winner's traffic for months, so any "before" window is a choice: the Oscar lift is {{oscarLiftPre}} against the 53 days ending a week before the nominations, {{oscarLiftSpec}} against the spec's window after them, and {{oscarLiftEarly}} against days -150 to -90 before the ceremony. The award sample is small ({{nAwards}} events, {{nBallon}} of them Ballon d'Or and {{nBooker}} Booker), so differences between prizes are descriptive.

Method

How it was built

Events
Wikidata SPARQL for deaths (run per half-year) and Academy Awards; the Nobel Prize API for laureates and announcement dates; each year's English Wikipedia article for Ballon d'Or and Booker dates. Queries in queries/, list in data/events.csv.
Pageviews
Wikimedia REST API, per-article/en.wikipedia/all-access/user/daily, days -60 to +365, titles resolved through Wikidata at fetch time; page moves inside the window found from revision summaries.
Measures
Baseline is the median of days -60 to -8 (for the Academy Awards, of the 53 days ending a week before the nominations), peak the maximum of days 0 to +3, half-life the days from the smoothed peak until the centered 7-day mean of excess views falls below half of it, lift the median of days +335 to +365 over baseline. Day-of-week factors from the baseline window.
Models
Baseline plus an exponential, power-law or biexponential decay, fitted to log(1 + daily views) on every day after the peak; AIC from the residual sum of squares; the selector checked on simulated series. The browser runs the same code: assets/fit.js is a port of scripts/fits.py, checked against it by scripts/check_fit_parity.py.
Regressions
OLS with HC3 errors of log half-life and log lift on event type, log baseline, occupation group and year; for deaths, also age at death. Full table in tables/regressions.csv.
Prior work
Candia, Jara-Figueroa, Rodriguez-Sickert, Barabási and Hidalgo (2019), The universal decay of collective memory and attention, Nature Human Behaviour 3, 82–91.
Built with
Python (pandas, NumPy, statsmodels, matplotlib); plain JavaScript and inline SVG for the charts. Every number on this page is written from the analysis outputs by scripts/export_numbers.py.
